Fit-Organiser  ·  Note Taker  ·  Design Directions
Six ways
to feel at home
Visual directions for a note-taking app — old-fashioned, warm, stylish, and quietly modern.

The brief, as I read it. Not retro-computing. Nothing grey, boxy or institutional. The reference points are analog and tactile — paper, ink, cloth, leather, wood — the objects people actually kept notes in before apps existed.

Every direction below follows four rules: warm paper-tone backgrounds instead of white or grey; one saturated accent, used sparingly; a real typographic voice with a serif or typewriter face for body text; and softness in the details — small radii, hairline rules, gentle shadows, no hard chrome.

“A bit modern” shows up as restraint rather than pastiche: generous spacing, a clear type hierarchy, and modern interaction comfort. The result should read as a well-made object, not a costume.

The idea

Utilitarian but affectionate. The kraft header is the cover; scroll and you're on the dot-grid page. Notes sit as slightly raised cards so the list feels like loose leaves tucked inside.

Body copy in a typewriter mono keeps it honest and unfussy, while Futura in the chrome does the modern work — geometric, clean, no nostalgia tax.

Feels like the notebook already in your back pocket — worn, unprecious, the one you don't mind writing a shopping list in.

The idea

The most premium and personal of the six. The dark hide header frames cream pages; gold hairlines do all the accenting; the crimson ribbon replaces any modern highlight state.

A drop cap and a small ornament give each entry the dignity of a page in a real journal — which quietly encourages longer, more thoughtful writing.

Feels like the hardbound notebook someone gave you as a gift — the one you hesitate to write the first page in.

The idea

The one with a real organising metaphor built in — cards, drawers, alphabet tabs, “see also”. Structure comes for free, which suits a notes app that might grow tags or folders later.

Ruled feint lines behind the text and a red rule under each header do the heavy lifting; the typewriter face is used at every size, so the whole thing holds together with one font.

Feels like the wooden drawer at a small-town library — slightly musty, entirely trustworthy, everything findable.

Chalkboard · Charter · Marker Felt

The idea

The warmest and most domestic option, and the most literal reading of “feel at home”. Colour-coded category pills, scalloped card edges, and a handwriting face reserved strictly for margin notes.

Worth knowing: the handwriting font is the risk. Used for one accent per screen it's charming; used for body text it becomes a novelty app fast. I'd cap it hard.

Feels like your grandmother's recipe box on the counter — splattered, annotated, unmistakably lived in.

The idea

The most modern-feeling of the six while still being unmistakably of another era. Warmth comes from the palette and the bone paper rather than from texture, so it stays crisp and very legible.

Colour-coded squares carry categories, hairline rules replace card borders, and the three-colour bar is the only flourish. Also the easiest to build and the easiest to keep consistent as the app grows.

Feels like a well-kept writing desk — the good pen, the nice paper, everything squared up and to hand.

The idea

The writer's option. Everything is a page: numbered, word-counted, with a red margin rule and generous line spacing that makes long-form typing genuinely pleasant.

Its trick is treating edit history as visible craft — strikethroughs in red pencil, insertions in handwriting, highlighter for the bit you're unsure about. Beautiful, and the narrowest in appeal: monospace body text is a real acquired taste on a phone.

Feels like a half-finished draft left in the typewriter — the one you keep coming back to.

Summary

Where I'd land

All six meet the brief. They differ in how loud the costume is, and in how well they'll hold up once the app has features.

Direction Warmth Modern Effort Best if…
Kraft & Ink HighMediumLow you want everyday, unprecious, quick-capture notes.
Leather & Cream HighLowMedium notes are long-form and personal — a journal more than a scratchpad.
Card Catalog MediumLowMedium organisation matters and you expect tags, folders, cross-references.
Recipe Tin HighestMediumMedium you want the friendliest, most domestic feel and don't mind whimsy.
Mid-century MediumHighLowest you want it to age well and stay legible as features pile up.
Manuscript MediumLowLow the app is for writing drafts, not lists.

Recommendation

Kraft & Ink, with two details borrowed from elsewhere: the mid-century colour-coded squares for categories, and the manuscript red margin rule in the editor.

It hits every word of the brief without tipping into pastiche. Kraft and dot-grid paper give real warmth; Futura in the chrome supplies the modern edge; the typewriter body face stays old-fashioned where it counts, in the writing itself. It also has the fewest ways to go wrong — no handwriting font to over-use, no dark leather to fight for contrast, no monospace tax on readability.

If you want warmer, take Recipe Tin. If you want it to look sharp for a decade, take Mid-century. Those are the two I'd argue for after my own pick.

What I still need from you

  1. Which direction — or a hybrid, if a specific pairing appeals more than any single page.
  2. What kind of notes is this really for: quick lists and capture, or longer writing? It changes the editor completely, and it changes which of these six is right.
  3. Platforms — iOS only, or do web and Android matter? All six work on the web, but bundled fonts and paper textures cost more there.
  4. Dark mode — I'd skip it for v1. Every one of these is built on warm paper, and the inversions are a design project of their own.
